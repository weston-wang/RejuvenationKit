# Small studies, shared controls and leaky references: calibration failures in aging-intervention analysis

*Methods note — draft for submission. Authors: RejuvenationKit contributors.*

## Abstract

Aging-intervention studies are small, multi-armed and measured with noisy multi-omic biomarkers.
Those are the conditions under which routine analysis choices fail silently. We audited common
analysis steps by simulation and on public data. Five failures stand out.

1. **Change detection.** Detectors calibrated on the same reference animals used to fit them
   raised a nominal 5% false-alarm rate to 9–41% at typical reference sizes. Leave-one-out
   calibration restored 3.5–5.5%.
2. **Random-effects fusion.** DerSimonian–Laird intervals across two to four biomarker
   modalities covered 76–88% at nominal 95%. Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman intervals covered at
   least 95.6%.
3. **Small-sample bootstrap.** Percentile intervals for group differences covered 81% with
   three animals per arm and 91% with eight. A variance-corrected Welch t interval covered 95–97%.
4. **Shared controls.** Fold-change vectors of two interventions measured against the same
   control animals correlate at about 0.5 when neither intervention has any effect. In public
   liver RNA-seq covering eight lifespan interventions (GSE131754), mean concordance across ten
   shared-control pairs fell from r = 0.24 to 0.07 once that artifact was removed, and no
   shared-control pair survived family-wise adjustment.
5. **Age acceleration.** Residualizing clock age on chronological age across all samples
   understated a true treatment effect by 38% in single-arm pre-post designs and by 50% when
   arms differed in age. Fitting the reference line on reference samples only was unbiased.

We also quantify how many independent trials a biomarker needs before it can be shown to be a
trial-level surrogate endpoint. For an excellent surrogate (true R²_trial = 0.81), 40 trials give
a 66% chance of showing R²_trial > 0.5.

All methods are implemented, with tests, in the open-source RejuvenationKit Python package.

## 1. Introduction

Interventions that slow aging in model organisms are typically evaluated in small studies:

- three to ten mice per arm;
- tens of companion dogs; or
- single-arm human pilot studies.

Endpoints are molecular clocks and omics signatures, because lifespan is slow and expensive to
measure. Three structural features recur:

- **Few animals per group.** Asymptotic intervals and resampling methods are near their breaking
  point.
- **Several arms against one control group.** Every between-arm comparison inherits the same
  control noise.
- **Clocks used as outcomes.** Age acceleration and "biological age reversal" are derived
  quantities whose reference line is itself estimated.

Individually these are well-known statistical facts. Our contribution is to measure how large
their effects are under realistic geroscience designs. We also provide corrected, tested
implementations and show that one correction changes a substantive conclusion on public data.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Out-of-sample calibration of multivariate change detectors

The detector scores a subject's change vector by its squared Mahalanobis distance from a
reference mean. The reference covariance is shrunk toward its diagonal, with a small ridge. The
threshold is the 95th percentile of reference scores. In-sample scores are biased low, because
each reference subject pulled the mean and covariance toward itself. The corrected method scores
each reference subject against a model fitted without it. We simulated standard-normal changes
with 20–517 reference subjects and 2–10 channels and measured the exceedance rate among 200
held-out subjects over 2,000 replicates.

### 2.2 Random-effects fusion intervals

Modality-level estimates with standard errors of 0.2–0.6 and between-modality SD τ ∈ {0, 0.5, 1}
were fused with DerSimonian–Laird weights. We compared two 95% intervals over 10,000 replicates:

- the Wald interval, which treats τ² as known; and
- the modified Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman interval, which scales the SE by √max(1, q_HK) and
  uses t(k−1).

### 2.3 Small-sample group differences

For a treated-minus-control mean difference we compared two 95% intervals, over 4,000 replicates
of standard-normal data:

- the nonparametric percentile bootstrap; and
- a bootstrap of variance-corrected deviations, inflated by √(n/(n−1)) so that the bootstrap
  variance equals s²/n, combined with a Welch–Satterthwaite t critical value.

### 2.4 Shared-control concordance

For interventions A and B sharing control group C in each stratum:

\[
\operatorname{Cov}(\hat\Delta_A, \hat\Delta_B) = \operatorname{Cov}(\Delta_A, \Delta_B) +
\operatorname{Var}(\bar C).
\]

The corrected estimator works per feature and then pools across features:

1. subtract the per-feature shared-control noise \(s_C^2/n_C\), averaged over strata;
2. divide by each vector's signal variance, estimated as its total variance minus its mean
   per-feature noise variance; and
3. report nothing when either signal fraction is below 10%.

A disjoint-control cross-check never lets the two contrasts share a control animal. It averages
over control splits and needs no noise model. The corrected covariance is tested with a
within-stratum label permutation (exact under the complete null), and p-values are
Holm-adjusted over all pairs. Simulations used 2,000 features, two strata and three animals per
group, with 100 replicates.

The public reanalysis used GSE131754: 78 mouse liver transcriptomes covering acarbose, caloric
restriction, 17-α-estradiol, Protandim and rapamycin (shared controls), and GHRKO, Snell dwarf
and methionine restriction (each with its own controls). Genes with CPM > 1 in at least half of
samples were kept (11,619 genes, selected without group labels). Each intervention's log2-CPM
effect vector was averaged over its own age/sex strata, and 999 permutations were run per pair.

### 2.5 Leakage-safe age acceleration

Age acceleration is the residual of clock age on chronological age. We compared two fits:

- a regression across all samples; and
- a regression on reference samples only (controls or pre-intervention baselines), with
  reference samples scored leave-one-out.

In the simulation the clock equals age plus N(0, 1.5²), dogs were aged 8 ± 2 years, and the true
effect is −2 clock-years, over 300 replicates.

### 2.6 Trial-level surrogate evaluation

Following the meta-analytic framework of Buyse et al. (2000), the between-unit covariance of true
treatment effects on surrogate and outcome was estimated by the method of moments:

\[
\hat\Sigma_b = \operatorname{Cov}(\text{estimated effects}) - \overline{W}.
\]

From it, R²_trial = Σ²_{b,12}/(Σ_{b,11}Σ_{b,22}). Intervals come from a parametric bootstrap on
the signed trial-level correlation. In development simulations a nonparametric bootstrap over
units covered only 61% with six units. The planning simulation drew true unit effects with a
known correlation and set surrogate reliability to 0.8, with 150 replicates per cell.

## 4. Discussion

The common thread is that the reference distribution is estimated from data that also contain
the signal or the subject being scored. This happens in four places:

- a detector's null model includes the animals it scores;
- a random-effects interval treats estimated heterogeneity as known;
- two treatment contrasts reuse one control group; and
- an age-acceleration line is fitted through the treated samples.

Each makes results look more certain or more coherent than they are. The effects are large at
the sample sizes geroscience actually uses and fade only at sizes it rarely reaches, for example
517 reference animals in Table 1.

The shared-control artifact deserves particular attention. Multi-arm designs with a common control
group are efficient and widespread, and cross-intervention concordance is a common argument for
shared mechanisms of longevity. In GSE131754 the naive analysis suggests that all five
shared-control interventions, including rapamycin, share a hepatic response. The corrected
analysis does not. The concordance that survives involves caloric restriction and acarbose
paired with growth-hormone-axis mutants and methionine restriction, which were measured against
independent controls.

We reanalyzed a single dataset with a deliberately simple pipeline. We do not claim that the
source publication, which built its signatures from many datasets with a different model, is
affected. We do claim that anyone correlating fold changes across arms that share controls must
remove this term.

Table 7 has an uncomfortable implication for the surrogate-endpoint agenda. Showing that an
intervention's effect on a clock predicts its effect on healthspan requires dozens of independent
intervention–outcome pairs measured in the same subjects. Individual-level correlation with
mortality, the usual validation, is neither necessary nor sufficient. Our simulated "surrogate
paradox" had individual R² = 0.82 with no trial-level predictive value. Large multi-arm studies
that give every arm its own controls, or explicitly model shared controls, are the realistic path
to enough units.

## 5. Limitations

- Simulations use Gaussian data and linear effects. Heavier tails and nonlinearity would
  generally worsen the failures of asymptotic methods.
- The concordance correction removes shared-*animal* noise. It cannot remove batch effects shared
  by separately controlled arms.
- The permutation test for concordance is conservative when real but unrelated effects are
  present.
- The method-of-moments surrogate fit is transparent but not efficient. Likelihood-based
  bivariate random-effects models would be more precise with unequal unit sizes.
- The GSE131754 reanalysis uses three mice per group. Its null results mean "not detectable",
  not "absent".
